# Guitar — Shape of My Heart

#music/fingerstyle · a companion to [[Guitar]]

Sting, *Ten Summoner's Tales* (1993). The guitar figure was written by **Dominic
Miller**, who is credited as co-writer — worth knowing, because people call it
"the Sting riff" and it is really a guitarist's piece that a singer built a song
on top of.

It is the piece a lot of people learn fingerstyle *for*. Worth being clear about
why it is hard, because it is not the notes.

## What it is actually made of

Strip the song away and the figure is one device, executed very well:

**An arpeggiated chord sequence over a bass line that moves independently.**

That is it. The right hand rolls through the notes of a chord while the thumb
walks somewhere the chord shape does not follow. The magic — and the difficulty —
is that the two hands are doing arithmetic at different speeds.

Three things make that style sound the way it does:

1. **A held top note across changing chords.** One string rings the same pitch
   while the harmony moves underneath it. The ear hears a constant while
   everything else shifts, which is where the wistfulness comes from.
2. **A descending bass.** Bass lines that step down feel like gravity. Almost
   every piece that gets called "haunting" is doing this.
3. **Chords voiced to keep the common tone available** — which usually means
   partial shapes, not textbook grips. See [[Guitar — Beyond the triad]]: this is
   where sus and add voicings earn their keep, because they let a note stay put
   while the chord changes around it.

None of that is unique to this song. Learn to hear it and you have a key to a
whole genre.

## The right hand

Classical fingerstyle notation, which the licensed tab will use:

| Symbol | Finger | Usually plays |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **p** | thumb (*pulgar*) | 6th, 5th, 4th strings |
| **i** | index (*índice*) | 3rd string |
| **m** | middle (*medio*) | 2nd string |
| **a** | ring (*anular*) | 1st string |

**The skill is thumb independence.** The thumb must keep a steady pulse while
i-m-a do something rhythmically different above it. This is the actual wall, and
it is a coordination problem rather than a guitar problem — the same wall as
playing different rhythms with each hand on a piano.

The drill that builds it, and it is worth a fortnight on its own:

1. Anchor a simple chord — Am will do.
2. Thumb plays the 5th string on **every beat**. Metronome at 50 BPM. Nothing else.
3. Keep that going and add **i** on the off-beat. Only that.
4. When it is automatic, add **m**, then **a**.
5. Now change chord without the thumb faltering.

If the thumb hesitates when the chord changes, you are not ready for the piece.
That is not a discouragement — it is the actual prerequisite, and it is learnable
in weeks.

## The left hand

Two things worth knowing before you start:

- **The shapes are mostly partial.** Three and four-string grips, not full barres.
  If a transcription has you barring constantly, it is probably not the shape
  Miller uses.
- **Fingers stay down.** Much of the fluency comes from leaving one or two fingers
  planted through a change, exactly like the Cadd9 family in
  [[Guitar — Beyond the triad]]. Look for the note that does not move and anchor it.

## How to learn this — or any riff — by ear

Genuinely worth doing before buying the tab. You will learn more in an hour of
this than in a week of reading someone else's numbers.

1. **Find the tonic.** Hum the note the phrase resolves to. Find it on the
   guitar. That is Sa ([[Guitar — Sargam and the scale]]) — everything else is
   measured from there.
2. **Get the bass first.** Bass notes are slower, louder and easier to hear than
   the arpeggios above them. Write down just the bass line.
3. **Slow it down.** Any player that changes speed without changing pitch — the
   phone apps do this now. Work at 50–70%.
4. **Loop two bars.** Not the whole piece. Two bars, thirty times.
5. **Add one note per chord.** With a bass note and one more, the shape is usually
   forced — there are not many chords containing both.
6. **Separate the hands.** Learn the fretting changes silently, with no picking.
   Then practise the picking pattern on open strings, with no fretting. Then join
   them. Trying to learn both at once is what makes people conclude they are
   untalented.

## Practising it

The method from [[Guitar — Rhythm and tempo]], and it applies here more than
anywhere:

- Start at a tempo where it is **perfect**, not one where it is nearly right.
  That will feel humiliatingly slow. Do it anyway.
- Four clean repetitions, then **+5 BPM**.
- When it falls apart, **drop 10** and stay there for a day.
- Play it **without the metronome once a session**, to check it still breathes.

The figure is not fast. Nobody is impressed by speed here, and rushing is the
single most common way it gets ruined — the piece depends on space.

## What it gives you afterwards

The reason to learn it is not the party trick. It is that once the thumb is
independent, an enormous amount of music opens up: Travis picking, most acoustic
singer-songwriter material, classical studies, and the ability to accompany
yourself without a band.

This piece is a good teacher because it is **slow enough to hear your own
mistakes** and hard enough to be worth the fortnight.
