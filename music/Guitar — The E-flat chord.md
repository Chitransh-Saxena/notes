# Guitar — The E♭ chord

#music/chords · a companion to [[Guitar]]

E♭ is the chord that stops people. Not because it is theoretically difficult —
it is an ordinary major triad — but because the guitar is built in a way that
makes it awkward, and nothing about the shape explains why.

## What it actually is

Three notes, like every major triad ([[Guitar — Chords]]):

| | Note | Interval | Sargam (Sa = E♭) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| root | **E♭** | 1 | **Sa** |
| third | **G** | major 3rd, 4 semitones | **Ga** |
| fifth | **B♭** | perfect 5th, 7 semitones | **Pa** |

**Sa · Ga · Pa.** Identical to C major, identical to G major, identical to every
other major chord. Only the starting pitch differs — which is the whole lesson of
[[Guitar — Sargam and the scale]] arriving in a place it is inconvenient.

## Why the guitar makes it hard

Look at the open strings: **E A D G B E**. Five of the six common beginner chords
— E, A, D, G, C — can borrow one or more of them, which is why they ring and why
they are easy.

**E♭ is not an open string, and neither is B♭.** There is nothing to borrow.
Every note must be stopped by a finger, so E♭ is a barre chord or a partial shape,
with no beginner-friendly version hiding anywhere. That is the entire difficulty,
and it is a property of the tuning rather than of the chord.

> [!note] This is why flat keys feel alien on guitar
> Guitar favours **sharp** keys — E, A, D, G — because its open strings are in
> them. Brass instruments are pitched in **B♭ and E♭**, so horn-driven music —
> soul, funk, big band, a great deal of jazz — lives in flat keys. Guitarists find
> that music awkward for a reason that has nothing to do with the music.

## E♭ or D♯?

Same fret. Different name. From [[Guitar — Sharps, flats and the twelve]]: the
key decides.

- **E♭** in flat keys — B♭ major, E♭ major, A♭ major, C minor. Almost always.
- **D♯** in sharp keys — as the leading note of E major, for instance.

You will see **E♭** perhaps fifty times for every **D♯**, because the keys it
belongs to are the flat ones. In sargam neither problem exists: with Sa = C it is
simply **komal Ga**, and there is nothing to argue about.

## The shapes

### The one to learn — A-shape barre, 6th fret

```
        6 5 4 3 2 1
E♭      x 6 8 8 8 6      E♭ B♭ E♭ G B♭
```

Root on the **5th string, 6th fret**. This is the standard voicing, and it is
easier than a barre at the 1st fret: the frets are narrower and the string tension
lower that far up the neck.

### The one to use when you are stuck — partial, no barre

```
        6 5 4 3 2 1
E♭      x x 1 3 4 3      E♭ B♭ E♭ G
```

This is the **open D shape moved up one fret**. Four strings, no barre, all three
notes of the triad present. It sounds thinner than the barre and it works
perfectly well in a mix. Learn this the day you need E♭ and the barre the week
after.

### And for completeness — E-shape barre, 11th fret

```
        6 5 4 3 2 1
E♭      11 13 13 12 11 11
```

Root on the 6th string. Useful when the music is already up there; a long reach
otherwise.

### Minor and seventh

```
E♭m     x 6 8 8 7 6      E♭ B♭ E♭ G♭ B♭     ← third flattened one fret
E♭maj7  x 6 8 7 8 6
E♭7     x 6 8 6 8 6
```

The minor is the barre shape with one finger lifted — the same single-semitone
change that separates every major from its minor ([[Guitar — Chords]]).

## Or avoid it entirely, honestly

From [[Guitar — The capo]] — a capo makes E♭ available from shapes you already
own:

| Capo | Play the shape | It sounds |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1** | D | **E♭** |
| **3** | C | **E♭** |
| **6** | A | **E♭** |
| **8** | G | **E♭** |
| **11** | E | **E♭** |

**Capo 3 and C shapes** is the one to remember. A singer who needs E♭ is not
asking you to learn a barre chord; they are asking for a different Sa, and a capo
at 3 gives them one in two seconds while your hands keep playing C, F, G, Am.

## The key of E♭ major

Three flats — **B♭, E♭, A♭**:

```
E♭   F    G    A♭   B♭   C    D    E♭
Sa   Re   Ga   Ma   Pa   Dha  Ni   Sa′
```

The same offsets `0 2 4 5 7 9 11 12` as always. Its chords, from the pattern in
[[Guitar — Chords]]:

| Degree | I | ii | iii | IV | V | vi | vii° |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Chord | **E♭** | Fm | Gm | A♭ | B♭ | Cm | Ddim |

Major, minor, minor, major, major, minor, diminished — unchanged, as it is in
every key.

## Where E♭ turns up when you are not in E♭

This is the useful part, because most of the time you meet E♭ it is a guest:

- **IV in B♭ major** — extremely common in horn music
- **V in A♭ major**
- **♭III in C major** — the borrowed chord. C → E♭ → F → C has a lift to it that
  a diatonic progression cannot produce, and it is the reason E♭ shows up in
  rock songs otherwise entirely in C.
- **The relative major of C minor** — the two share all three flats, so any piece
  in C minor is a step away from E♭ the whole time.

In sargam terms that last group is the interesting one: in the key of C, an E♭
chord is **built on komal Ga**. Borrowing the flattened third of the scale as the
root of a major chord is a very old trick and it still works.

## How to actually get it under the fingers

1. Play the partial `x x 1 3 4 3` until it is clean. Use it in real playing today.
2. Practise the **A-shape barre at fret 6**, not at fret 1. Easier, and it is the
   same shape.
3. Change **B♭ → E♭ → B♭** (`x 1 3 3 3 1` → `x 6 8 8 8 6`). Same shape, sliding
   five frets. Barres get easier from moving, not from squeezing.
4. Then **A♭ → E♭ → B♭** — the IV–I–V of E♭ major, all A-shapes at frets 4, 6
   and 1... which is when the point lands: **one shape, the whole key.**
