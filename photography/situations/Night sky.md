# Night sky

#photo/nightsky · Note 6 of 7 · part of [[Photography]]

The 35mm f/1.8 is a genuinely good astro lens — fast, and at ~56mm equivalent
tight enough to pick out the Milky Way core without needing a tracker.

## The 500 rule, and why it is wrong

The old rule: maximum exposure seconds = **500 / effective focal length**.

For this kit: 500 / (35 × 1.6) = 500 / 56 ≈ **8.9 seconds** before stars trail.

That rule was written for film. On a 24MP APS-C sensor, examined at 100%, stars
smear noticeably sooner. The **NPF rule** accounts for pixel pitch and aperture
and gives roughly **5–6 seconds** for this combination.

In practice: **shoot 6 seconds**, check at 100%, and go to 8 if you are printing
small or your standards are kind.

## The settings

- **Manual mode**, **manual focus**
- **f/1.8**, or **f/2.2** if the corner stars look like little wings — that is
  coma, and stopping down one third of a stop cleans it up considerably
- **6 seconds**
- **ISO 3200** to start; 6400 if the sky is genuinely dark
- **White balance ~3900K**, not auto — auto will chase the light pollution
- **RAW**, always
- **IS OFF.** It is on a tripod. See [[Low light]].

## Focusing on stars

Autofocus will fail. Do this instead:

1. Live view, digital zoom to **10×**
2. Point at the brightest star or a distant light
3. Turn the focus ring until the star is the **smallest** point, not the
   brightest — go past it and come back
4. **Tape the focus ring** so a knock doesn't undo it

## What you need besides the camera

- **A tripod.** Non-negotiable — there is no handholding a six-second exposure.
- **A remote or the 2-second timer.** Pressing the shutter shakes the camera.
- **A dark sky.** This matters more than any setting. Bortle 4 or darker; from
  Bangalore that means driving out, ideally towards Coorg, Kolar, or anywhere
  away from the city glow.
- **A red torch**, so twenty minutes of dark adaptation survives.

## Stacking, when one frame is not enough

Noise is random; the sky is not. Shoot **15–20 identical frames** plus a few
"dark" frames with the lens cap on, then average them:

- **Sequator** (Windows, free) or **Siril** (cross-platform, free, open source)
- **Starry Landscape Stacker** on macOS (paid, excellent)

The result is roughly what ISO 400 would have looked like if it had gathered that
much light. It is the single biggest quality jump available without a tracker.

## Foreground

A sky on its own is a wallpaper. Something in front of it — a ridge, a tree, a
building — is a photograph. Expose the sky first, then take a second, longer
frame for the foreground and blend the two.
